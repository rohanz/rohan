## the conception

The <a href="/projects/quantlab-analyst">memo project</a> ended with a system that could write financial memos where every number was mechanically provable. But it had a ceiling built into its shape: the model wrote from a fixed <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="A pre-computed bundle of facts handed to the model. It never chooses what to look at; someone else already decided.">evidence pack</span>. It never *decided* what to look up. Real analyst work is the deciding: which filing, which year, which companies to compare, what to compute. So the question this time: **can a small model do the looking-up itself, and can you trust what comes back?**

That second clause carries the whole project. LLMs are confident about numbers and unreliable about them, and in finance an unverifiable number is worse than no number, because someone acts on it. Frontier models don't fix this. They get more numbers right, but you still can't audit where any of them came from. The goal, then: a small open model (<span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Qwen3.5-9B, Alibaba's 9-billion-parameter open model (Apache 2.0), small enough to run on one GPU. The base for everything trained here.">9 billion parameters</span>, runs on one GPU, could live inside a bank's walls) that answers research questions by querying SEC filings through tools, computing explicitly, and citing everything. Trained so that answering from memory takes an explicit, unavoidable grounding penalty.

## the environment is the product

Before any training, the model needs a world to act in, and most of the engineering went here. Three layers.

**A point-in-time database.** 12.5 million facts from SEC filings across 495 companies, each keyed to the date it was actually filed. Ask what was known in March 2021 and you get March-2021 knowledge, with <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Companies frequently revise past figures in later filings. A restated number is the corrected version; the originally-reported one is what the market saw at the time. Both are 'true', for different questions.">restatements</span> handled explicitly and nothing leaking backward from the future. Honest backtests have this discipline; this applies it to question-answering, which is exactly where generic LLM-plus-web-search setups go wrong.

**Six tools.** Fundamentals lookups, point-in-time queries, price history, return and volatility stats, a screener over the universe, and a calculator that accepts only arithmetic on literal numbers. The paranoia is deliberate: a model being trained with <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Reinforcement learning: the model tries things, a reward function scores them, and behaviors that score well get reinforced. The model will find any loophole the reward leaves open.">RL</span> is effectively an adversary probing your sandbox. Every tool returns structured errors that a confused model can read and recover from.

**5,500 questions whose answers are computed, never labelled.** Four difficulty tiers: lookups, derived math, screens, multi-step chains. The question generator calls the same tools the model will use, so a question only exists if it is answerable and unambiguous. Woven through them are **trap questions** about companies that restated their figures, where the number everyone remembers is wrong and only the filings give today's answer. There are also point-in-time questions sampled inside <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The window between two companies' filing dates, where one has reported a new fiscal year and the other hasn't. Inside it, 'who reported higher revenue as of date X' can flip depending on whether you honor the date.">asymmetric filing windows</span>, verified per-question so that ignoring the "as of" clause produces the wrong answer.

One of the five is a failure, because a benchmark that only shows wins is marketing.

## a reward that pays for evidence

Everything the model learns, it learns from one function: the reward. Ours is pure code, with no <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Using a second LLM to grade the first one's answers. Popular, but judges carry biases (verbosity, family favoritism) and can't be audited either.">LLM judges</span> anywhere, and it grades four things: the answer matches the tool-computed gold (70%), tool calls are well-formed (10%), the episode doesn't waste calls (10%), and **grounding** (10%), meaning the stated number must appear in the transcript's tool evidence. A correct but unverifiable answer takes a strict penalty. In this domain, *how you know* is part of *whether you're right*.

A reward that an RL loop will optimize against needs the same treatment as security code, so it went through adversarial review before training. A second model family attacked it and found five real exploits: computing your memorized answer into fake legitimacy, farming empty-set questions, a denial-of-service in the calculator, scale tricks against the evidence matcher. Two follow-up review rounds found that *fixes* had opened fresh holes, with the reviewer executing live exploits to prove it. By the time training started, the reward had survived everything we could throw at it. It still wasn't perfect: real transcripts later exposed two scorer bugs, visible because every model's grounding "collapsed" on one split at once. Cross-model uniformity is the tell. Models fail differently; scorers fail identically. We fixed the scorer, re-scored every stored transcript, and the tables in this article are the corrected ones.

## teaching the model

A warning before the numbers start. Every student-model score in this section and the two after it is the score we *believed* at the time, measured through a serving stack that, we later discovered, had a silent fault. The teacher and frontier baselines (served by their vendors' APIs) are unaffected. The chapter called "the week the ruler bent" re-measures everything, and the honest numbers change the story. I am leaving the believed numbers in place because watching them fall apart is the most instructive thing this project produced.

### auditioning a teacher

The training recipe is the standard 2026 pipeline: distill tool-use episodes from a frontier model, run <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Supervised fine-tuning: training the model to imitate example transcripts token-by-token. Teaches format and workflow; can't exceed its examples.">SFT</span> on the verified ones, then <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Group Relative Policy Optimization (DeepSeek's recipe): sample several answers per question, score each with the reward, push the model toward its better-than-average attempts. RL without a value network, cheap enough for one GPU.">GRPO</span> against the reward. But which teacher? We benchmarked frontier candidates on our own environment first, and the result set the tone for everything after: the flagship model lost to its own cheaper sibling, and lost hardest on the trap questions, where its stronger memory made it *more* confident in stale numbers. The mid-tier model, at half the price, won the audition. Even the base, untrained 9B beat the flagship on traps. Less knowledge means less to be wrong with. On an evidence-demanding scoreboard, confidence is a liability.

We kept only teacher episodes whose final answer and grounding both scored perfect, about 2,700 transcripts, so the student never imitated a wrong or unevidenced answer. For questions the teacher kept failing, a <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Append the known answer to the question as a hint, let the teacher work out a tool-grounded derivation of it, then strip the hint from the training copy. From the STaR line of work.">hint pass</span> recovered grounded demonstrations, with the hint stripped before training and the fraction capped, because a corpus of justifications teaches justification.

### imitation first, and the eleven-point flag

Supervised fine-tuning took the base model from a recorded 0.730 to a recorded 0.822, half the teacher gap in one step, and delivered what we then believed was the project's cheapest hard lesson. The first evaluation came back flat: 0.734, barely above base. Flipping the serving stack's <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Qwen models can generate hidden chain-of-thought before answering. Trained behavior and serving configuration must agree about whether this is on.">thinking mode</span> off produced a large apparent recovery, and we filed it as an axiom: train-time and serve-time distributions must match. The axiom is correct and this project would eventually prove it at a depth we did not intend. The ruler chapter later showed that both of those measurements were of the base model (the fine-tuned weights were not actually being served), so what the flag experiment really measured was base with and without thinking. The lesson survived; the evidence under it was replaced.

### reinforcement learning against the verifier

Then the main event: 300 steps of GRPO, where no teacher exists. The model explores, the reward scores, and the verifier itself becomes the teacher. Getting it running took an eight-launch shakedown (missing dependencies, a weight-name mismatch between the training and serving stacks, out-of-memory arithmetic at 16k context), each failure one layer deeper than the last. The recorded result: 0.829 overall, with the gains exactly where imitation couldn't reach. Multi-step chains rose 2.9 points and traps rose 1.6, and <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="If the model's outputs become deterministic too early, RL has nothing left to explore and learning stops. The classic GRPO failure mode.">entropy stayed healthy</span> for the whole run.

Here is where we believed the edge lived. The chart below opens on the recorded ladder: the orderly staircase we celebrated, including the headline we almost published, the RL-trained 9B overtaking its frontier teacher 0.883 to 0.852 on question types withheld from training. Toggle to the honest ladder, re-measured after the events of the ruler chapter, and the staircase rearranges: the base model was better than recorded, the SFT model much better, and the RL stage gave points back. The toggle between those two views is this article's real thesis.

## the agent takes over the research

With training working end-to-end, the last experiment was the most contemporary one: hand the hyperparameter research to an agent. The setup is Karpathy's autoresearch ratchet, adapted. A frozen 150-question metric slice, a config file of levers the agent may turn, a contract (one change per experiment, hypothesis written before launch, keep or revert by the metric, hard budget caps), and a driver that trains a short proxy run and scores it. The agent's first campaign produced zero experiments: it found and fixed a string of bugs in its own laboratory, then stopped, exactly per contract. Its second and third campaigns produced the finding. My hand-chosen learning rate was ten times too timid, corrected across three measured steps. The chart below shows its three successful experiments: one dial turned twice, the score climbing each time. The second view carries the finding that outlived the campaign.

<div id="qla2-ratchet"></div>

Promoted to a full run, the agent's recipe scored +1.5 on validation and *lost* 2.6 points on unseen question shapes. The single-slice metric it climbed predicted in-distribution gains perfectly and missed the generalization cost entirely. We had spent weeks hardening the reward against the policy gaming it, and the metric got gamed anyway, one level up, by the optimization process itself. <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Here: the proxy metric was optimized, and what it didn't measure (generalization) was traded away.">Goodhart's law</span> doesn't disappear when you automate the researcher. It moves. The v1 model kept the headline slot, per rules written before either run.

That was the lesson we thought we had learned about measurement. A larger one was waiting underneath it. The agent's operational story stands (it really did fix its own laboratory and hold to its contract, and its learning-rate finding held up in training reward, though it was never re-established on the fixed evaluator), but its evaluation layer sat on the same faulted serving stack as everything else, so its metric conclusions carry the same asterisk the next chapter removes.

## the week the ruler bent

It started with a $15 repair job. The evaluation transcripts showed the model mishandling fiscal-year lookups, so I built a small surgical dataset teaching the correct behavior, trained it, and measured no change. Trained it again with the fix everyone finds eventually (the chat template used in training must byte-match the one used in serving), and the behavior probe still showed the old habits, even on the model's own training questions. A model that cannot reproduce its own training data is not a stubborn model. It is a measurement problem. (That second checkpoint, once the measurement was fixed, turned out to be genuinely damaged: 0.433 with corrupted arguments. Both things can be true.)

The debugging that followed peeled six layers in two days, each layer producing a wrong conclusion that the next experiment killed:

<div id="qla2-cascade"></div>

The bottom layer is the one worth a job interview question. vLLM, serving our fine-tuned <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Low-rank adapters: the small trained weight deltas from fine-tuning, loaded on top of a base model at serve time.">LoRA adapters</span>, had been silently applying zero of their 256 tensors: the adapters were trained against the text-only model class while vLLM instantiated the multimodal one, the module names missed, and no error was raised. Proven with token-identical logprobs between "adapter" and base. Every student-model evaluation in the campaign had been measuring the base model in a costume. The recorded ladder (0.730, 0.822, 0.829) was base with the thinking flag in different positions, plus sampling noise.

And beneath that, one more layer, the deepest: with the adapter genuinely applied, the model still degraded, emitting junk tool arguments, until the rendered tool schemas matched the training corpus *byte for byte*. The frozen training file serialized schema properties alphabetically; the live code rendered them in code order. The two are equal as data structures, which is why the training-time guard (a value comparison) blessed them, and different as prompts, which is why the fine-tuned policy degraded. The base model shrugs off the difference; the specialist is brittle to it. That asymmetry is what made every broken measurement look plausible for weeks.

Trust was rebuilt from the bottom: a 300-line serving harness in plain transformers with an auditable tool-call parser, pinned by tests that assert byte equality between the live schemas and the frozen corpus. Then the whole ladder was re-measured through it. The honest numbers: base 0.790, SFT 0.899, GRPO 0.871 (full validation set, training temperature). Three reversals in one table. The SFT stage, undersold by eight points, had quietly built an excellent model and taught exactly what its data contained (fiscal-year discipline went from 0.540 to 0.940). The RL stage, the chapter with the headline, gave back 2.8 points net: total return chains fell from 0.919 to 0.751, absence screens from 0.674 to 0.545, and on aggregate screens (0.634 base, 0.430 after fine-tuning) training actively hurt, the regressions concentrated where the training loop's parsing contract had been subtly lenient. And the "weakness" the $15 surgery was designed to fix did not exist in the real model. I had been about to spend money repairing a phantom created by the measurement.

What survived, and it matters that these survived: the training runs themselves (their on-policy rewards flowed through a correctly-shimmed path), the environment, the reward, the teacher baselines, and the operational lessons. What died was every claim that depended on serving a fine-tuned checkpoint through the faulted path. The project's headline is no longer "a $65 model beats its teacher". It is "SFT built a 0.899 model, RL slightly damaged it under a bent ruler, and the instruments now exist to know either way". The second claim is smaller and worth more.

## production serving

Training a model is half the job; running one is a discipline of its own, and this project treats it as part of the result. The serving stack is the same one industry runs (vLLM, containerized, Kubernetes manifests with a canary rollout in the repo), and the benchmarks below were measured on it, not estimated. First, the engine choice, since the two projects together earn an opinion: the memo project served on <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="A developer-friendly wrapper around llama.cpp: trivially easy local model running. The right tool for trying models on your own machine.">Ollama</span> and this one on <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The de-facto industry inference engine: continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching, multi-adapter serving, OpenAI-compatible API.">vLLM</span>, and the rule of thumb is: Ollama to try models, vLLM to serve them. This project needed what Ollama doesn't have. Continuous batching for 16-plus concurrent RL rollouts, maintained tool-call parsers for the model family, and one base model hosting multiple <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Low-rank adapters, the small trained weight deltas from fine-tuning. vLLM can serve several on one loaded base model, like profiles.">LoRA adapters</span> at once.

The chart below compares serving throughput two ways: the synthetic benchmark everyone reports, and our real agentic workload replayed against the same server. The benchmark that matters is the one most write-ups skip.

<div id="qla2-bench"></div>

Synthetic token throughput climbs happily to 32 concurrent streams. Real agentic episodes saturate at concurrency around 16 and then degrade, because multi-turn tool loops are round-trips, not generations. If you serve agents, your capacity math is different from your chatbot's. These timing results survived the ruler chapter (throughput is throughput regardless of which weights answered), but the FP8-versus-bf16 *quality* comparison ran through the faulted serving path, so the honest version of that claim is narrower: <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="8-bit floating point, half the bytes of bf16. The production default on modern GPUs.">FP8</span> gave 40% more throughput, and its quality parity needs re-measuring on the fixed stack before it graduates from plausible to claimed.

The deployment absorbed the ruler chapter's lessons as requirements. The serving image is built in CI, pinned, and pushed to a private registry; pods boot from the image with versioned weights on a mounted volume; the checkpoint being staged for serving is the merged SFT champion, merged offline into the full model layout precisely so that no runtime LoRA machinery can silently no-op again; and the tool schemas are byte-normalized at the source, with a regression test that fails the build if the rendered prompt ever drifts from the training corpus. The canary rollout tier is manifests-in-repo, not yet a live cluster, and this article will say so until it is one.

## what this is

The whole project cost in the region of $350, of which a traceable $45.50 went on the forensic week that mattered most. The failure catalog cost extra in dignity (a 59GB memory leak that made three overnight runs look stalled, a GPU-host lottery, one run killed by a dropped SSH pipe, a tunnel that died silently mid-evaluation) and lives in the project's append-only journey log, because that is where the reusable knowledge is.

What the project actually demonstrates, in the order I would defend it in an interview. Building an environment where correctness is computable: point-in-time data, generated questions with computed answers, a code-only reward that pays for evidence and survived adversarial review. Running the full 2026 training stack hands-on: teacher distillation, SFT, GRPO on rented GPUs, with the failure modes of each stage experienced rather than read about. Operating production-shaped serving: pinned CI-built images, versioned weights on volumes, canary manifests, capacity numbers measured on real agentic load. And the one that binds them: refusing a flattering number. The project's best result was detected because a cheap probe (can the model answer its own training questions?) disagreed with a dashboard, and the follow-through replaced a false headline with a true, smaller, better-instrumented one. Evaluation integrity is a skill you can only demonstrate by catching yourself.

The recipe generalizes past finance: tools over trusted data, questions whose answers are computed, rewards that pay for evidence, and measurement contracts pinned to the byte, because the fine-tuned models your training produces are exactly the ones most brittle to drift your value-equality tests cannot see. Anywhere "how do you know?" matters more than eloquence, this is the shape of the answer.
