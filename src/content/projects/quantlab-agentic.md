## the conception

The <a href="/projects/quantlab-analyst">memo project</a> ended with a system that could write financial memos where every number was mechanically provable. But it had a ceiling built into its shape: the model wrote from a fixed <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="A pre-computed bundle of facts handed to the model. It never chooses what to look at; someone else already decided.">evidence pack</span>. It never *decided* what to look up. Real analyst work is the deciding: which filing, which year, which companies to compare, what to compute. So the question this time: **can a small model, trained as an agentic orchestrator, do the looking-up itself, and can you trust what comes back?**

That second clause pretty much informed the whole project. LLMs are confident about numbers and unreliable about them, and in finance an unverifiable number is worse than no number, because someone acts on it. <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The biggest commercial models of the day (GPT, Claude, Gemini class): the strongest available, rented through an API.">Frontier models</span> don't fix this. They get more numbers right, but you still can't audit where any of them came from. The goal, then: a small open model (<span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Qwen3.5-9B, Alibaba's 9-billion-parameter open model (Apache 2.0), small enough to run on one GPU. The base for everything trained here.">9 billion parameters</span>, runs on one GPU, could live inside a bank's walls) that answers research questions by querying SEC filings through tools, computing explicitly, and citing everything. Trained so that answering from memory takes an explicit, unavoidable grounding penalty.

## the environment is the product

Before any training, the model needs a world to act in, and a lot of the engineering went here. Three layers.

**1. A point-in-time database.** 12.5 million facts from SEC filings across 495 companies, each keyed to the date it was actually filed. Ask what was known in March 2021 and you get March-2021 knowledge, with <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Companies frequently revise past figures in later filings. A restated number is the corrected version; the originally-reported one is what the market saw at the time. Both are 'true', for different questions.">restatements</span> handled explicitly and nothing leaking backward from the future. Truthful backtests have this discipline; this applies it to question-answering, which is exactly where generic LLM-plus-web-search setups go wrong.

**2. Six tools.** These are the only ways the model can touch the data, the same way a plugin or API is the only way a trading system touches an exchange. One looks up a company's <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The accounting numbers a company reports: revenue, net income, assets, debt and so on.">fundamentals</span>, one answers "what was known as of this date", one fetches price history, one computes return and volatility statistics, one <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Ranks every company in the dataset by a chosen metric and returns the top of the list, like a stock screener.">screens</span> the whole universe, and one is a calculator that accepts only arithmetic on literal numbers, so the model cannot smuggle a remembered figure in as an "input". That last restriction sounds a little paranoid, but that's exactly the point: a model being trained with <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Reinforcement learning: the model tries things, a reward function scores them, and behaviors that score well get reinforced. The model will find any loophole the reward leaves open.">RL</span> is rewarded for score, not honesty, so it will lean on any gap in the rules the way water finds cracks. Designing the tools as the only means of interacting and producing data means closing these cracks first. And when the model does call a tool incorrectly, the tool answers with a structured error message it can read and recover from, instead of crashing the episode.

**3. 5,500 questions, with answers computed.** Four difficulty tiers: lookups, derived math, screens, multi-step chains. The question generator calls the same tools the model will use, so a question only exists if it is answerable and unambiguous. Woven through them are **trap questions** about companies that restated their figures, where the number everyone remembers is wrong and only the filings give today's answer. There are also point-in-time questions sampled inside <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The window between two companies' filing dates, where one has reported a new fiscal year and the other hasn't. Inside it, 'who reported higher revenue as of date X' can flip depending on whether you honor the date.">asymmetric filing windows</span>, verified per-question so that ignoring the "as of" clause produces the wrong answer.

## evidence is always rewarded

Everything the model learns, it learns from one function: the reward. Mine is pure code, with no <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Using a second LLM to grade the first one's answers. Popular, but judges carry biases (verbosity, family favoritism) and can't be audited either.">LLM judges</span> anywhere, and it grades four things: the answer matches the tool-computed gold (70%), tool calls are well-formed (10%), the episode doesn't waste calls (10%), and **grounding** (10%), meaning the stated number must appear in the transcript's tool evidence. A correct but unverifiable answer takes a strict penalty. In this domain, *how you know* is effectively part of *whether you're right*.

A reward that an RL loop will optimize against needs the same treatment as security code, so it went through adversarial review before training. A second model family attacked it and found five real exploits: computing your memorized answer into fake legitimacy, farming empty-set questions, a denial-of-service in the calculator, scale tricks against the evidence matcher. Two follow-up review rounds found that *fixes* had opened fresh holes, with the reviewer executing live exploits to prove it. By the time training started, the reward had survived everything I could throw at it. It still wasn't perfect, though: real transcripts later exposed two scorer bugs, visible because every model's grounding "collapsed" on one split at once. Cross-model uniformity is the tell. Models failed differently; scorers failed identically. I fixed the scorer, re-scored every stored transcript, and the tables in this article are the corrected ones.

## teaching the model

One note before the numbers: every score in this article comes from a re-measurement made late in the project, after I discovered and fixed a silent fault in my original evaluation setup. There is a short section on that hiccup below, because it taught more than the training did. The numbers here are the ones that survived it.

### auditioning a teacher

The training recipe is the standard 2026 pipeline: <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Have a stronger model solve your tasks, keep its good transcripts, and train the smaller model to imitate them. The strong model is the teacher, the small one the student.">distill</span> tool-use episodes from a frontier model, run <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Supervised fine-tuning: training the model to imitate example transcripts token-by-token. Teaches format and workflow; can't exceed its examples.">SFT</span> on the verified ones, then <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Group Relative Policy Optimization (DeepSeek's recipe): sample several answers per question, score each with the reward, push the model toward its better-than-average attempts. RL without a value network, cheap enough for one GPU.">GRPO</span> against the reward. But which teacher? I benchmarked frontier candidates on my own environment first, and the result set the tone for everything after: the flagship model lost to its own cheaper sibling, and lost hardest on the trap questions, where its stronger memory made it *more* confident in stale numbers. The mid-tier model, at half the price, won the audition. Even the base, untrained 9B beat the flagship on traps. Less knowledge means less to be wrong with. On an evidence-demanding scoreboard, confidence is a liability.

I kept only teacher episodes whose final answer and grounding both scored perfect, about 2,700 transcripts, so the student never imitated a wrong or unevidenced answer. For questions the teacher kept failing, a <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Append the known answer to the question as a hint, let the teacher work out a tool-grounded derivation of it, then strip the hint from the training copy. From the STaR line of work.">hint pass</span> recovered grounded demonstrations, with the hint stripped before training and the fraction capped, because a corpus of justifications teaches justification.

### imitation first

Supervised fine-tuning is where the model actually got good: from 0.790 to 0.899 on the <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Questions held out of training and used only for measurement, so the score reflects ability rather than memorization.">validation set</span>, and it taught exactly what its training data contained. The clearest example is fiscal-year discipline: asked for "the fiscal year ending on date X", the base model reaches for the wrong lookup and scores 0.540 on those questions; the SFT model scores 0.940. That habit was taught, not innate, and it is precisely the habit a finance workload needs.

### reinforcement learning against the verifier

Then the main event: 300 steps of GRPO, where no teacher exists. The model explores, the reward scores, and the verifier itself becomes the teacher. Getting it running took eight launches, each one dying a layer deeper than the last: missing dependencies, then a weight-name mismatch between the training and serving stacks, then out-of-memory arithmetic at 16k context. The training itself ran cleanly: on-policy rewards climbed from 0.68 to 0.74 and <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="If the model's outputs become deterministic too early, RL has nothing left to explore and learning stops. The classic GRPO failure mode.">entropy stayed healthy</span> the whole run.

And the honest result is that it didn't help. The RL model scores 0.871: small gains on lookups and traps, paid for with larger regressions on multi-step chains (total return fell from 0.919 to 0.751, absence screens from 0.674 to 0.545), concentrated exactly where the training loop's tool-call parsing had been subtly lenient. A negative result with a mechanism attached is still a result, and it is written up as one. ## the agent takes over the research

With training working end-to-end, the last experiment was the most contemporary one: hand the hyperparameter research to an agent. The setup is Karpathy's autoresearch ratchet, adapted. A frozen 150-question metric slice, a config file of levers the agent may turn, a contract (one change per experiment, hypothesis written before launch, keep or revert by the metric, hard budget caps), and a driver that trains a short proxy run and scores it. The agent's first campaign produced zero experiments: it found and fixed a string of bugs in its own laboratory, then stopped, exactly per contract. Its second and third campaigns produced the finding: my hand-chosen learning rate was ten times too timid, corrected across three measured steps. The chart below shows its three successful experiments: one dial turned twice, the score climbing each time. The second view carries the finding that outlived the campaign.

Promoted to a full run, the agent's recipe scored +1.5 on validation and *lost* 2.6 points on unseen question shapes. The single-slice metric it climbed predicted in-distribution gains perfectly and missed the generalization cost entirely. I had spent weeks hardening the reward against the model gaming it, and the metric got gamed anyway, one level up, by the optimization process itself. <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Here: the proxy metric was optimized, and what it didn't measure (generalization) was traded away.">Goodhart's law</span> doesn't disappear when you automate the researcher. It moves. The v1 model kept the headline slot, per rules written before either run.

One asterisk: the agent's scoreboard sat on the same faulted evaluation setup as everything else (next section), so its operational story stands while its metric conclusions stay provisional.

## the measurement hiccup

For most of the project my evaluations were quietly measuring the wrong model. vLLM, serving my fine-tuned <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Low-rank adapters: the small trained weight deltas from fine-tuning, loaded on top of a base model at serve time.">LoRA adapters</span>, was silently applying zero of their weights (a naming mismatch between two versions of the model's code, with no error raised), so every "fine-tuned" score was actually the base model in a costume. Underneath that sat a second, subtler fault: the fine-tuned model is brittle to the exact byte layout of the tool definitions in its prompt, and my evaluation rendered them in a different order than training had. I caught it with a cheap probe (the model could not reproduce answers from its own training data, which is a measurement problem, not a model problem), spent two days peeling the layers, and rebuilt the evaluation as a small serving harness I could audit line by line, pinned by tests that assert byte equality with the training corpus. Every number in this article comes from that rebuilt harness.

## production serving

Training a model is half the job. Running one is a discipline of its own, and this project treats it as part of the result. The serving stack is the same one industry runs (vLLM, containerized, <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The industry-standard system for running fleets of containers. A 'manifest' is the config file describing what to run.">Kubernetes</span> manifests with a <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Releasing a new model to a small slice of traffic first, watching it, and only then rolling it out fully. Named after the canary in the coal mine.">canary rollout</span> in the repo), and the benchmarks below were measured on it, not estimated. First, the engine choice, since the two projects together earn an opinion: the memo project served on <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="A developer-friendly wrapper around llama.cpp: trivially easy local model running. The right tool for trying models on your own machine.">Ollama</span> and this one on <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="The de-facto industry inference engine: continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching, multi-adapter serving, OpenAI-compatible API.">vLLM</span>, and the rule of thumb is: Ollama to try models, vLLM to serve them. This project needed what Ollama doesn't have. Continuous batching for 16-plus concurrent RL rollouts, maintained tool-call parsers for the model family, and one base model hosting multiple <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Low-rank adapters, the small trained weight deltas from fine-tuning. vLLM can serve several on one loaded base model, like profiles.">LoRA adapters</span> at once.

The chart below compares serving throughput two ways: the synthetic benchmark everyone reports, and my real agentic workload replayed against the same server. The benchmark that matters is the one most write-ups skip.

<div id="qla2-bench"></div>

Synthetic token throughput climbs happily to 32 concurrent streams. Real agentic episodes saturate at concurrency around 16 and then degrade, because multi-turn tool loops are round-trips, not generations. If you serve agents, your capacity math is different from your chatbot's. One honesty note here too: the throughput numbers are solid (throughput is throughput regardless of which weights answered), but the <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="8-bit floating point, half the memory of the standard 16-bit (bf16) format. Smaller numbers, faster serving, possible quality cost.">FP8</span>-versus-bf16 *quality* comparison ran through the faulted evaluation path, so the claim stays narrow: FP8 gave 40% more throughput, and its quality parity needs re-measuring before it graduates from plausible to claimed.

The deployment treats the hiccup's lessons as requirements, not suggestions. The serving image is built in <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="Continuous integration: the automated build system that runs on every code push, on neutral infrastructure rather than someone's laptop.">CI</span>, pinned, and pushed to a private registry; pods boot from the image with versioned weights on a mounted volume; the <span class="gloss-term" data-gloss="A saved copy of the model's weights at a specific point in training. 'The SFT checkpoint' means the model as it was at the end of supervised fine-tuning.">checkpoint</span> being staged for serving is the merged SFT champion, merged offline into the full model layout precisely so that no runtime LoRA machinery can silently no-op again; and the tool schemas are byte-normalized at the source, with a regression test that fails the build if the rendered prompt ever drifts from the training corpus. The canary rollout tier is manifests-in-repo, not yet a live cluster, and this article will say so until it is one.

## what this is

The whole project cost in the region of $350, of which a traceable $45.50 went on the measurement forensics that mattered most. The failure catalog cost extra in dignity (a 59GB memory leak that made three overnight runs look stalled, a GPU-host lottery, one run killed by a dropped SSH pipe, a tunnel that died silently mid-evaluation) and lives in the project's append-only journey log, because that is where the reusable knowledge is.

What the project actually demonstrates, in the order I would defend it in an interview. Building an environment where correctness is computable: point-in-time data, generated questions with computed answers, a code-only reward that pays for evidence and survived adversarial review. Running the full 2026 training stack hands-on: teacher distillation, SFT, GRPO on rented GPUs, with the failure modes of each stage experienced rather than read about. Operating production-shaped serving: pinned CI-built images, versioned weights on volumes, canary manifests, capacity numbers measured on real agentic load. And the one that binds them: refusing a flattering number. The project's best result was detected because a cheap probe (can the model answer its own training questions?) disagreed with a dashboard, and the follow-through replaced a false headline with a true, smaller, better-instrumented one. Evaluation integrity is a skill you can only demonstrate by catching yourself.

The recipe generalizes past finance: tools over trusted data, questions whose answers are computed, rewards that pay for evidence, and measurement contracts pinned to the byte, because the fine-tuned model your training produces is exactly the one most brittle to the kind of drift your equality tests cannot see. Anywhere "how do you know?" matters more than eloquence, this is the shape of the answer.
